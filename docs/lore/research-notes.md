# Research notes: The Blacktongue books as a loose model for Relic's writing

Compiled 2026-11-01 for the Relic writing guide. Sources at the end. Short quoted fragments are
reviewers' or blurb words unless marked otherwise; nothing here reproduces passages from the novels.
Confidence tags: [firm] = several sources agree; [one source] = single review, recap or fan post;
[fan] = Reddit or fan analysis, treat as reader interpretation.

---

## 0. Which book did Kyle mean by "the goblin wars book"?

- **Most likely: The Blacktongue Thief (2021)** itself. The Goblin Wars are not a book there; they
  are the recent past that shapes everything (dead men, dead horses, veterans, a ruined north).
  Kinch's narration refers back to them constantly. [firm]
- **Strong second: The Daughters' War (June 25, 2024, "Blacktongue #0").** A prequel that is
  literally a goblin-war novel: Galva's war memoir from the last, bleakest phase of the wars.
  If Kyle remembers goblin battles on the page, sieges, war birds and women soldiers, this is the
  one. [firm]
- **New: The Thrice-Bound Fool (October 13, 2026, "Blacktongue #2").** Direct sequel to Thief,
  out about three weeks ago. Kinch acquires a stolen book with a mind of its own; Galva guards him;
  the thieves' guild still hunts him. Few reviews yet; blurbs from Mark Lawrence and Christian
  Cameron. Probably not what Kyle meant, but worth knowing it exists. [one source for premise]
- Reading order: chronological is Daughters' War, Blacktongue Thief, Thrice-Bound Fool;
  publication is Thief, Daughters' War, Thrice-Bound Fool.
- **Other books actually titled "Goblin Wars" (none plausibly fit):**
  - Kersten Hamilton, *The Goblin Wars* trilogy (Tyger Tyger 2010, In the Forests of the Night
    2011, When the Stars Threw Down Their Spears 2013): YA paranormal romance in modern Chicago,
    Irish folklore, the Sidhe. Wrong tone entirely.
  - Stuart Thaman, *The Goblin Wars* (Siege of Talonrend, Death of a King, etc.): self-published
    epic fantasy, a goblin shaman breaks free of his queen's hive mind. Obscure.
  - Jim C. Hines, *Goblin War* (2008, third of the Jig the Goblin trilogy): comic parody told
    from a goblin's side. Funny, but goblins as heroes, opposite of Relic.
- Conclusion: "The Blacktongue Thief and its goblin wars," with The Daughters' War as the deep
  source for goblin warfare. Recommend Kyle confirm, and that the writing guide draw on both.

## 1. The books' facts

### The Blacktongue Thief (Tor, May 25, 2021)
- **Premise:** Kinch Na Shannack, a young thief, owes the thieves' guild (the "Takers") for his
  training, a crushing student-loan style debt marked by a tattooed hand on his face. A roadside
  robbery goes wrong: the target, Galva, a knight and goblin-war veteran, kills his whole crew. The
  guild then orders Kinch to attach himself to her and travel with her on a long overland and sea
  journey north. She is searching for a missing queen in a northern land overrun by giants. Kinch
  slowly learns the guild's darker purposes. [firm]
- **Narrator:** Kinch, first person, telling the story after the fact like a man at a tavern table.
  He has a gift for languages, uncanny luck, and dodged the war draft, which veterans resent. [firm]
- **Supporting cast types** (names in section 6, do not reuse): a death-goddess knight with a giant
  war crow; a young apprentice witch (Kinch's romance); a bitter hometown veteran turned whaler who
  despises Kinch for dodging the war (their prickly bond is often called the most convincing
  relationship in the book); a tattooed guild assassin who shadows him; an independent crone witch;
  a bone-sorcerer who makes animal-human hybrids; a blind tabby cat that follows Kinch impossibly far
  and matters to the plot. [firm]
- **Setting basics:** A broken post-war continent. Celtic-flavored home culture (the author calls
  them a "lost tribe" rather than real Celts), an Iberian-flavored knightly kingdom, a cold northern
  whaling nation that stayed neutral in the war and now trades with goblins, a northern land fallen
  to giants. Roads are dangerous, horses nearly extinct, donkeys and walking instead. [firm]
- **Magic:** Iron and steel dampen magic; copper and natural materials amplify it. Spells are either
  cast freely by witches and guild magickers or "caught" in tattoos and objects (a staff that becomes
  a clockwork horse for an hour, assassins' tattoos that make heat or speed). Deliberately limited so
  no mage can beat an army. [firm, author interview]
- **Gods:** A crowded pantheon. A sly fox god of thieves and luck (Kinch's patron, who values being
  amused over piety); a gaunt death goddess served by knights; a wolf war god; an all-god of the sun
  that soldiers and nobles swear by; a sea god; a mercy goddess; a stag god; a hidden "Forbidden
  God" the guild secretly serves. Oaths are built from gods. [firm]
- **Monsters besides goblins:** giants (with castes, honor and shrinking magic), krakens,
  horse-sized war ravens, mixling hybrids (a bull-man outlaw), witches. [firm]

### How the Goblin Wars figure in (both books)
- **Three waves of goblin war** (author, Den of Geek). Fan recaps name them the Knights' War, the
  Threshers' War and the Daughters' War. By the last wave so many men were dead that women fought
  it and won at great cost. [firm on three waves; one source for the names]
- **Aftermath in Kinch's time:** most adult men dead; women hold most power and most soldiering
  roles (the author says he wanted to show how artificial patriarchy is and how easily it flips);
  horses wiped out by a goblin-made plague; a generation of veterans with trauma; resentment
  against the neutral nation that traded with goblins and now keeps a chained-off goblin quarter in
  one of its cities. [firm]
- **Dread of the next time:** The author builds dread around the possibility that humanity could
  lose if the goblins come again. Peace is a pause, not an ending. [firm, author]
- **War crows:** Goblins fear birds, even small ones. Humans bred giant, magically altered, mostly
  flightless war corvids to fight them, partly as revenge for the horse plague. After the war,
  rulers ordered the birds slaughtered; one knight kept hers. Veterans read this as rulers discarding
  the tools and soldiers that saved them. [firm, author plus recaps]
- **The Daughters' War (book):** Galva at about twenty, a duke's daughter, youngest of four, defies
  her family to join an untested women's unit of war-crow riders. Told as her journal or memoir,
  plus letters and a brother's journal. Bleak, military, logistics-minded (armies lose to hunger
  and thirst before battle). Two of her three brothers die to goblins. Grief is the dominant theme;
  small moments of love and humor keep it bearable; ending hopeful. Much less funny than Thief.
  [firm]

### What goblins are like in that world [mix of firm and fan]
- **Alien, not D&D goblins.** Reviewers stress they are "truly alien," unlike Tolkien or D&D
  goblins, and possibly "not of the world." Fans note they do not rot and must be burned; they may
  have come up from deep underground after an ancient cataclysm. [firm on alien; fan on origin]
- **Body:** small (fans say about a meter), asymmetrical, one claw longer for digging, deliberately
  ugly to human eyes; hook-handed. [fan / recap]
- **Society:** hive-like, coordinated as one body; rumored to send commands through the earth;
  ruled by a queen in a single monolithic state. They fight without regard for death until the
  group breaks. They keep their bargains, even with humans. [fan plus recap]
- **Humans as livestock:** The stated war aim is to "make cattle of mankind." They eat people,
  farm them, tithe conquered towns into human farms and hobble captives by cutting a tendon, make
  goods (even sails) from human skin, and breed a domesticated, drug-addicted human subspecies used
  as battering rams. [firm on eating and cattle; fan/excerpt on details]
- **Way of war:** competent and cunning, not a mindless swarm. Poisoned crossbow bolts that punch
  plate, poison gas, nets and billhooks, sappers tunneling under walls, boar-drawn bladed chariots,
  war horns meant to spread fear, sickening sigils, strong weather magic (a goblin mage turns a
  witch's wind against her), germ warfare against horses. They avoid iron because it grounds magic.
  [excerpt plus fan]
- **Psychological terror:** an elite order wears human faces as masks and worships a moth; their
  priests blow nightmare spores and rattle human teeth; war engines are hung with human skins.
  [firm, FanFiAddict]
- **Speech:** In the Daughters' War excerpt, goblins speak in a flat, rough, clipped, limited way.
  Kinch speaks some of their tongue (languages are his gift). Soldiers use euphemisms and nicknames
  for them rather than the word goblin, including an ironic polite one. [excerpt]
- **Culture glimpses:** a ritual tug-of-war contest to the death in the neutral city (fans say it
  was rigged); a captured goblin ship whose crew eats a sailor. [recap]
- **Critique to note:** Grimdark Magazine felt they "lack the nuance" of good villains and "kill
  for the sake of it"; the same reviewer found the worst villains are "closer to home." Most readers
  instead call them among the most terrifying fantasy enemies they have read. [firm]

### Themes [firm across sources]
- War's long shadow: trauma, survivor resentment, draft dodgers vs veterans, a population of widows
  and daughters.
- Debt and institutions: the guild controls through debt and public humiliation; Kinch's
  loyalty is coerced, and the plot is about choosing whom he really owes.
- Honor as a luxury: rigid honor codes kill people (the veteran dies of pride in a pointless
  contest); Kinch's flexible ethics are set against them.
- Luck and faith: Kinch treats risk as worship of his trickster god.
- Mercy within brutality: he rescues a cat, spares a would-be killer, falls in love for a month.
- Complicity: trading with the enemy, rulers who discard their soldiers.
- Companionship and song: songs carry news and morale; companionship balances the grim.

## 2. Style and tone, concretely

### Blacktongue Thief (Kinch's voice)
- **Tavern storyteller frame.** He talks to "you," promises to explain later, admits he exaggerates
  or lies and winks while doing it. The author describes him as a man at a pub table enjoying the
  best and worst of his own story. [author, excerpt]
- **Digressive.** Frequent asides (how towns grow, how a card game works, what luck is), and world
  facts dropped mid-action in passing references rather than info dumps. Grimdark Magazine says
  pausing action for worldbuilding helps rather than hurts. [firm]
- **Profanity level: high but inventive.** Earthy period swearing (arse, shyte, plus modern
  four-letter words) mixed with invented insults; curses differ by culture; one oath is adapted from
  Spanish. The author was a professional Renaissance-faire insult comic ("Christophe the
  Insultor"), which shaped the barbs. One reviewer found the crude, scatological mode outnumbered
  the lyrical one; others loved it. [firm]
- **Comic similes.** Crude, physical, homely comparisons (drunk as a pickled fish; sadness like
  heavy grey bricks). Aphorisms about power and class (truth as a luxury of the powerful). Gods
  mocked for being good only at easy things. [quotes page]
- **Humor amid horror ("grindark").** The author's own coinage: the world has collapsed, but the
  narrator's "wiseassery" and "relentless gallows humor" make it bearable. First person was chosen
  so the jokes could soften violence. Reviewers stress the humor never cancels the weight. [author]
- **Horror craft.** Buehlman came from horror (Between Two Fires, Those Across the River, The
  Lesser Dead). He compares horror to strict-form poetry: dread must recur on a steady rhythm.
  Several scenes read as horror rather than fantasy. [author, reviews]
- **Violence:** fast, brutal, matter-of-fact; fights end quickly and badly; consequences linger.
- **Sad moments:** delivered plainly, often undercut or followed by a joke, but not erased; grief
  shows up as small details (a dead stepbrother mentioned in passing, a veteran's pointless death).
- **Pacing:** episodic road story, one danger after another with little breathing room; reviewers
  call it hard to put down.
- **How characters talk to each other:** clash of registers. Kinch is crude and quick; the knight is
  stiff, formal, honor-bound and laconic, and will argue the grammar of a swear. Banter is a
  buddy-movie engine. The veteran and Kinch snipe with old hometown grudges.
- **Songs and verse:** ballads and rebel songs appear in the text (sung in the audiobook); songs
  carry news. The author lists faire musicians as writing influences.

### The Daughters' War (Galva's voice)
- Formal, old-world, aristocratic diction; terse and stoic, sometimes "stilted" or "emotionless,
  but unflinching." Calm while describing horror. Self-aware asides about her own bias.
- Memoir/journal frame with letters and a brother's diary in between; aphoristic, reflective.
- Soldiers' talk is rough camp slang: nicknames for women soldiers, unit and rank words borrowed from
  Spanish-sounding terms, nicknames for goblins, card games in camp, oaths by the sun god.
- Far less humor; grief-heavy; "gut-punching." Shows the same world from inside the war.

## 3. What makes the characters memorable

- **The lovable rogue who is softer than he admits.** Kinch: lucky, mouthy, cowardly by
  reputation, quietly kind (the cat, mercy to an enemy). Readers love him; some find him grating.
- **The grim knight with a code.** Galva: few words, devout to death, fiercely loyal to her
  principles more than to people; her loyalty to Kinch reads as principle first, affection later.
- **The resentful veteran.** Hometown man who fought while Kinch dodged; contempt, then grudging
  alliance, then a death that hurts.
- **The cheerful practical witch.** Young, optimistic, raw talent; romance that some found rushed.
- **Ambiguous institutions as characters.** The guild is a menace with ranks and rituals; its
  assassin is both ally and jailer.
- **Animal companions with personality.** The blind cat and the war crows (protective, carrion
  eaters, a bird that fakes pain to annoy people) are fan favorites. The corvid bond is praised as
  among the best animal companionships in fantasy.
- **Moral greyness everywhere:** heroes steal, lie and kill; rulers betray soldiers; "neutral"
  neighbors profit from the enemy; family feuds rival the war for pettiness (Daughters' War).
- **Common criticism to learn from:** trust and romance form too fast (Publishers Weekly,
  Fantasy Literature); the knight felt underdeveloped from Kinch's view.

## 4. Comparable works reviewers name, and how Blacktongue differs

- **Scott Lynch, The Lies of Locke Lamora:** the most frequent comp (thieves, profanity, banter).
  Grimdark Magazine called Blacktongue the best fantasy debut since it. Difference: Blacktongue is a
  road quest through a war-ruined world, not a heist city; one narrator, not an ensemble con.
- **Nicholas Eames, Kings of the Wyld / Bloody Rose:** buddy quest with humor. Blacktongue is darker
  and more horror-tinged.
- **Joe Abercrombie:** the author names him as a major influence. Blacktongue has similar grit but
  more warmth and wonder; the author rejects pure "grimdark" for "grindark."
- **Glen Cook, The Black Company:** Cook blurbed it ("Damned good stuff"); the war-memoir angle of
  Daughters' War sits close to Black Company's soldier's-annals voice.
- **Martin McDonagh / In Bruges:** the author's own comparison for the narrator's company: violent,
  profane, darkly funny, sad underneath.
- **Peter McLean, War for the Rose Throne:** distinctive first-person criminal voice.
- **Others named:** George R.R. Martin, Neil Gaiman, Patrick Rothfuss (author's fantasy
  influences), Stephen King (horror root), Don Quixote (Galva's devotion), D&D thief characters
  (Kinch's origin), Tyler Whiteside's Ardor Benn.
- **Pratchett:** not named by any source I found. Any Pratchett resemblance (footnote-like asides,
  satire of institutions) is mine, not reviewers'.
- **What is distinctive:** horror-writer dread inside a comic voice; a gender-flipped post-war
  society; goblins as the most frightening thing in the book rather than fodder; worldbuilding
  through offhand references.

## 5. Goblin inspiration for Relic (spirit, not copy)

Behaviors and menace worth borrowing in principle:
- **Goblins as competent, alien, organized.** Not comic grunts. They plan, keep bargains, use
  poison, tunnels, nets and fear. A raid should feel like walking into a working enemy system.
- **Humans as livestock** gives the rescue jobs real horror: villagers taken alive, pens, tallies,
  hobbled captives. Keep it implied and in props (empty pens, cut ropes, tally marks) rather than
  gore, which suits a phone game and leaves room for jokes.
- **Fear as a weapon:** horns before a raid, trophies on walls, masks, smells that tell you they are
  near. Telegraph menace through sound and set dressing before combat.
- **One hive, many hands:** goblins that do not flee individually but break all at once; a "butcher"
  as a node the hive relies on, so killing it collapses a den.
- **A specific weakness** that the town exploits (the books use birds). Relic should invent its own
  (bells, salt, firelight, dogs, a smell, a song) so the town's tactics have flavor.
- **Speech:** sparse, flat, clipped, unsettlingly practical. Goblin lines work best as short
  commands, prices and counts, not snarling villain monologues. Townsfolk calling them by
  euphemism or nickname rather than "goblin" is a cheap, strong tone tool (invent Relic's own).
- **The peace that is not peace:** a neighbor who trades with them; a quarter where they live under
  truce. Good fuel for council votes (trade or burn?).
- **Aftermath texture for Wickhold:** missing men, missing horses or other loss that changed daily
  life, veterans vs those who stayed home, widows running things. Invent Relic's own losses.

Tone recipe that fits a co-op raid game:
- Banter in town, dread in the dens, a joke after the scare, never a joke over a grave.
- Each hero gets a register (crude rogue, formal knight, cheerful practical caster, bitter veteran
  are the Blacktongue set; Relic's Warrior, Ranger, Healer, Mage can borrow the contrast idea
  without these exact personalities).
- Short, earthy oaths built on Relic's own gods; homely comic similes; songs as news.

## 6. Things to AVOID copying

**Proper names (characters, creatures, places):** Kinch Na Shannack, Galva (Galvicha), Norrigal,
Malk, Sesta, Deadlegs (Guendra), Mireya, Fulvir, Yorbez, Hornhead, Bully Boy, Dalgatha (bird and
goddess), Bellu, Amiel, Pol, Pettrec, Ürmehen, Misfa, Bavotte; Galtia/Galts, Ispanthia, Gallardia,
Manreach, Molrova, Grevitsa, Oustrim, Hrava, Holt, Norholt, Pigdenay, Cadoth, the Forest of Orphans,
the White Road, the Downward Tower, the Thrall Mountains.

**Invented terms:** Takers Guild and its ranks (Prank, Fetch, Faun, Famine, Scarecrow; Problems,
Worries, Shadows, Full Shadows), Debtor's Hand, Murder Alphabet, sleeper tattoos, witness coins,
Magickers Guild, Assassin-Adept, Coldfoot Spear, musterman, mixlings, springwood shield, Old Kesh;
kynd, biters, "Our Friends," "Our Foe," dams; godsmilk, Hordelaw, manfarms, the Stumbles, ghalls,
Moth Knights, juggernauts, the Knock; Raven Knights, Knight Ravens, corvid corps, lanza, daguera;
the Knights' War, Threshers' War, Daughters' War; the card games Towers and Catch the Lady; god
names Fothannon, Sath, Solgrannon, Haros, Mithrenor, Cassa, Tuur, Nerene, the Forbidden God,
the Allgod. Also "grindark" (the author's coinage).

**Signature ideas and plot beats to transform rather than lift:**
- A thief bound by tuition debt with a debt tattoo on the face; a guild that farms orphans.
- A blind cat companion with a hidden secret.
- Giant bred war crows as the anti-goblin weapon, goblins' fear of birds, and the postwar slaughter
  of the birds. (Pick a different weakness and a different veteran-betrayal.)
- A goblin-engineered plague that wiped out horses, and a giant used as a living ark of extinct
  animals.
- Specific scenes: the ritual tug-of-war death, the captured goblin ship eating a crewman, poisoning
  through the salt, skin-faced moth-worshipping knights, human-skin sails.
- The exact gender-flip premise (most men dead, women now rule) as the central social fact. A war
  that changed who holds power is fine; Wickhold should get its own version (for example a town
  run by whoever came back, or by the young).
- Iron dampens magic and copper amplifies it; tattoos as caught spells.
- A death-goddess knight as a lead type; a Celtic "lost tribe" plus Iberian knightly kingdom map.

**Safe to share in spirit (genre commons):** goblins that eat people, a walled last town, veterans
and draft dodgers, a thieves' guild, witches, krakens and giants as monsters, trickster and death
gods, tavern-storyteller narration, profane banter, gallows humor, songs as news.
