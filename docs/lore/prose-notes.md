# Prose notes: how the Blacktongue books actually read

Notes from reading the published excerpts closely, for how the writing moves on the page rather than
what it's about. Read in November 2026:

- **The Blacktongue Thief, chapter 1** (Tor/Forge blog excerpt): Kinch opening, the ambush in the
  wood, meeting Galva.
- **The Blacktongue Thief, a later chapter** (Reactor excerpt): Kinch in Cadoth, the card game, the
  fight, the ring, luck, the Guild's letter.
- **The Daughters' War, the opening chapters** (Nerd Daily excerpt): Galva on ship with the birds,
  her commander, her brother's letter.

Only the sound is ours to learn from. Nothing in here (names, terms, scenes, jokes) goes into Relic;
`research-notes.md` section 6 still holds.

## 1. What the prose actually does

### It talks, it doesn't announce
The narrator is someone at a table telling you a story, and he knows you're there. He says *you see*,
*mind you*, *I'm not ashamed to tell you*, *I won't bore you with*, *more about that later*. He
promises to come back to things and sometimes does. The reader is a person being talked to, not an
audience being addressed.

### Most sentences are long, and they wander on purpose
Roughly six long sentences to every four short ones. The long ones carry the explaining, the
describing and the remembering: clause after clause joined by commas and *and*, a list, a side
thought, then back. The short ones are rarer than you'd think and they do the hitting: a confession,
a reveal, the end of a fight, the last word of a joke. Because they're rare they land.

A typical paragraph: a plain claim or a concrete thing, a long sentence that builds on it with
examples, a side road, a qualification, then a short line at the end that turns it.

### He qualifies himself
*Or at least I think I did. If I didn't miss my guess. I might be biased. For the record, it wasn't.*
He's unsure, he exaggerates and admits it, he corrects himself. Galva in The Daughters' War does the
same in a stiffer register: *though I am not unbiased*, *forgive me if I tell you too much*. That
hedging is what makes it sound like a person and not a narrator.

### People and places arrive by a detail or an opinion, and the name comes after
Someone shows up as a nickname, a body, a smell or a verdict, and only then gets a name and a
history, and the history is a flat line or two (*he used to be a farmer*). Places arrive the same
way: what they're like to stand in, what's wrong with them, then what they're called.

### The world is mentioned, not explained
Names of peoples, wars, gods and things appear with no gloss at all. When something does get
explained, it's because the narrator feels like it, and he does it like a man explaining a card game
in a pub: a general claim, then *say you've got a village with one mud road*, a little imagined
example, an exception, a joke at the end. The explanation is a story about the thing.

### Jokes come at the end, and they're about bodies and people
The funny part closes the sentence or the paragraph, on a deflating last clause. The humour is
crude, physical and specific: what someone smells like, what they did to themselves, what they're
bad at. It's never a pun on the situation and never a wink at the reader about the genre.

### Sad things go plain and quiet
When it's sincere, the slang drops out and the sentences get simpler: a blunt confession, a fact,
one longer quieter sentence about what was lost. Then a joke comes along soon after, and the sadness
is undercut but never taken back. Galva handles horror by listing what she saw in calm, even
sentences and letting the list do the work.

### Spoken lines are short, and they assume you know
Out loud, people say very little: an order, a name, an insult, a word of warning. They don't explain
themselves; the listener is expected to know, and the narrator fills in later if he bothers. People
answer the wrong question, misread each other, talk across each other. A lot of the talk is
reported rather than quoted (*he was telling me to...*). Accents are described, not spelled out,
apart from a word or two.

### Connecting words
*Now. See. So. Mind you. By the way. For the record. As for. Of course. Though. Also. Anyway.*
They're how the voice gets from one thought to the next without a heading or a bullet. They make it
meander, and meandering is fine.

### Openings and endings
A chapter opens on the voice, not the plot: two short lines that tell you who's talking and what
kind of trouble they're in. A section ends leaning forward: a plan, a need, a line that says
something just changed.

## 2. Where Relic's writing has been going wrong (the Claude tells)

Read against the books, our lines sound like someone writing *lines*. The tells:

1. **Every sentence is a punchline.** Three or four short declaratives in a row, each one trying to
   land. *"Home. Kettle's older than you and better company. Hold on. I never leave that door open."*
   The book would make one of those a long, wandering sentence and let only the last one hit.
2. **Aphorisms on tap.** Little sayings that sound wise and tidy: *"Eleven you burn. One I keep."*
   *"Meet the blow as it lands, not before."* One of these in a whole scene is character; one a line
   is a fortune cookie.
3. **Fragments as style.** *"Up, slow." "Odric. Give him the count." "Good steel. Gold first."*
   Fragments are fine for a terse speaker in a hurry. Used everywhere, they're a tic, and every
   character starts to sound the same (clipped, laconic, cool).
4. **Too direct, too finished.** Each line states its point cleanly and stops. Nobody hedges, forgets,
   corrects themselves or wanders off. Real people circle a thing before they say it.
5. **The tidy three.** *"Soldier or thief, they took your boots."* Lists of three, balanced pairs,
   colon reveals. They read as constructed.
6. **Teaching in the character's mouth.** *"Hit it, and keep hitting till it's quiet."* is Hew doing
   the tutorial's job. In the book, nobody explains a mechanic; they'd tell you about the last fool
   who didn't do it.
7. **UI voice that sounds like a game.** *"Red with a striped edge can't be blocked. When it's about
   to land, roll out of it."* This is fine for the plain instruction line, and it should stay plain;
   but it shouldn't leak into the characters.

## 3. What to do instead

- **Let people talk at length when they're the talking kind.** Hew is an old charcoal burner alone
  in the woods who's found someone to talk to; he should run on a bit, lose the thread, come back to
  it. Halvard stays terse because Halvard is terse. Contrast between speakers is the point.
- **One long sentence, then one short one.** Build with commas and *and*. Save the short line for the
  end, and only when something should land.
- **Hedge and correct.** *I think. Or near enough. Mind, I might be wrong.* Let a speaker take back
  half of what they said.
- **Lead with the detail, then the name.** *"The big one with the burnt ear, that's Dunn."*
- **Explain by story, not by rule.** Instead of how a thing works, a short story about someone who
  got it wrong.
- **Joke at the end, about a body or a person.** Not about the game, not about the situation.
- **Sad: plain, then leave it.** No adjectives. Then something ordinary.
- **Out loud, assume the listener knows.** Don't name the war, the god, the town to someone who
  lives there.
- **Use the joining words.** *Now, see, mind you, anyway, as for.* Let it meander a little.
- **The plain instruction line stays plain.** What the player must press is told in the game's own
  voice, short and clear, separate from the character's words. The character never does the
  explaining.

## 4. Text that explains the game

Kyle, November 2026: the game text is "too much and too little", it "uses obscure language instead
of saying what things do", and it reads in "short, stilted sentences instead of direct and
understandable". Standing notes: less is more; we overwrite. These rules are for every line that
tells the player how something works: knacks, perks, item traits and mods, potions, relic words,
shop and store text, the trainer, class cards, lessons, tips, options, status names, toasts,
callouts, the job board, party and lobby text, and the church, council and relic panels.

- **Say what it does, in plain words.** Name the effect, and the number when the number matters:
  "+20% damage for 5 s", "Heals friends near you for 8".
- **No riddles or invented jargon.** A knack, perk, item trait or button says its effect, not a
  poetic phrase. Flavour goes in a separate, optional line, if anywhere.
- **Short and direct** (see Kyle's style below). One or two short sentences per thing, or a clear
  fragment; never a paragraph. A list only when there really are separate effects.
- **Explain once, in the right place.** Don't repeat a rule on the card, the hint, the tip and the
  toast. Pick the place the player looks when they need it and say it there.
- **Names should hint at the effect.** QUICK STEP, not an obscure noun. If you need the card text to
  know what the name means, the name is wrong.
- **Keep the two voices apart.** The world's people (dialogue) keep their voice, but they must still
  be clear about anything the player needs to act on. Game text (cards, knacks, buttons, tips,
  stats) is plain, second-person and direct: "you", "your", "friends near you".
- **Use the same word for the same thing everywhere.** Stamina (not breath or wind), and MAGIC for
  a Mage's same bar (Kyle, 2026-11-04: a Mage reads MAGIC everywhere the bar is named), PERFECT (a hold
  let go on the white flash; there's no perfect block), health, damage, friends, foes. If a word means a mechanic on one screen, it
  means the same mechanic on every screen.
- **One white flash.** "The white flash" is a hold's cue: let go on it (a bow's draw, the
  spellblade's enchant, every "ON THE FLASH" in the kit lines). The telegraph on the floor never
  flashes white now (parry went on 2026-11-05), so "the red flashes white" is retired, and no line
  says PARRY, RIPOSTE or PERFECT PARRY.
- **Mercy stays off screen.** The word is reserved for the Unmaking in the story bible.

### Kyle's style (the model for game text; Kyle, 2026-11-04)

Kyle's own copy notes are the style guide: "see my writing style and how I'm being to the point, and
let's emulate that." He writes a few words, the effect first, no flourish. Fragments are fine when
they're clear. Lines of his to copy the shape of:

- "Heavy armor and effective blocking."
- "Blinks away to dodge."
- "Divine Savior: Cannot harm allies"
- "Train skills at your trainer. You can only have one active skill at a time."
- "Fed by the coals left behind by butchers. Permanent increase to healing, but increases enemy aggression."
- "From the shadows, fast and fragile."
- "Tap: Short sword / Hold: Draw bow / Perfect: Blinds."

So: the name, a colon, what it does. No "you'll find that", no wind-up, no joke at the end of a
rule. Weapon rows are two to four words each. When you rewrite anything near a line he wrote (a class
card, a weapon row, GUARD and DODGE, a skill, a knack), match it. Dialogue keeps a character's voice,
but says the thing the player needs in the first sentence or two, just as plainly.
